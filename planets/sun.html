01 / INTRODUCTION

Meet Our Nearest Star

The Sun is a nearly spherical ball of hot plasma. It produces energy through nuclear fusion in its core and contains more than 99.8% of the total mass of the Solar System.

What is the Sun?

The Sun is a G-type main-sequence star, often called a yellow dwarf. It formed approximately 4.6 billion years ago from a cloud of gas and dust.

Its gravitational pull keeps Earth and the other planets in orbit. Energy generated by fusion in the Sun's core travels outward and eventually escapes into space as sunlight and other radiation.

The Sun is not a solid object. Its visible surface is called the photosphere, and its outer atmosphere extends millions of kilometres into space.

02 / STRUCTURE

Inside the Sun

The Sun has several internal layers and atmospheric regions. Each plays a role in how energy is produced and released.

01

Core

The central region where nuclear fusion converts hydrogen into helium and releases energy.

02

Radiative Zone

Energy moves outward mainly through the absorption and re-emission of radiation.

03

Convective Zone

Hot plasma rises, cools, and sinks, transporting energy toward the Sun's surface.

04

Photosphere

The visible layer from which most of the sunlight we see originates.

05

Chromosphere

A layer of the Sun's atmosphere above the photosphere, visible under certain observing conditions.

06

Corona

The Sun's extremely hot outer atmosphere, extending far into interplanetary space.

03 / COMPOSITION

What Is the Sun Made Of?

Hydrogen and Helium

The Sun consists primarily of hydrogen and helium. Under the immense pressure and temperature in its core, hydrogen nuclei combine through nuclear fusion to form helium.

This process releases the energy that ultimately reaches Earth as sunlight. Smaller amounts of heavier elements, including oxygen, carbon, neon, and iron, are also present.

Why Does the Sun Shine?

Deep inside the Sun, nuclear fusion releases energy. That energy gradually travels through the Sun and escapes from its surface as electromagnetic radiation.

Sunlight supports photosynthesis, warms Earth's surface, and drives much of our planet's weather and climate system.

04 / SOLAR ACTIVITY

A Star Full of Activity

The Sun is constantly changing. Its magnetic field creates a variety of powerful and fascinating phenomena.

⚫

Sunspots

Darker-looking regions on the photosphere that are cooler than their surroundings because of strong magnetic fields.

⚡

Solar Flares

Sudden releases of energy from the Sun that can produce intense radiation and affect conditions in near-Earth space.

🌬️

Solar Wind

A continuous stream of charged particles flowing outward from the Sun into the Solar System.

🔥

Solar Prominences

Large structures of relatively cool, dense plasma suspended in the Sun's atmosphere by magnetic fields.

🌀

Coronal Mass Ejections

Large eruptions of plasma and magnetic field that travel outward from the Sun.

🌈

Solar Cycle

The Sun's magnetic activity rises and falls over a cycle averaging about 11 years.

05 / OUR CONNECTION

Why Earth Needs the Sun

The Source of Life's Energy

  • Light: Provides the energy plants use for photosynthesis.
  • Heat: Helps maintain temperatures that allow liquid water to exist on Earth's surface.
  • Weather and climate: Uneven solar heating drives atmospheric and ocean circulation.
  • Space weather: Solar activity can affect satellites, radio communications, and power systems.
  • Gravity: The Sun's gravitational influence keeps Earth and the other planets in orbit.

Never look directly at the Sun without proper solar-viewing protection. Ordinary sunglasses are not safe for viewing a solar eclipse or the Sun directly.

Fascinating Facts About the Sun

🌌

One Star Among Billions

The Sun is one of the many stars in the Milky Way galaxy.

💡

Light Takes Time

Sunlight takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach Earth.

🪐

Solar System's Centre

The Sun contains about 99.86% of the Solar System's total mass.

🌡️

A Hotter Outer Atmosphere

The corona can reach millions of degrees Celsius, much hotter than the photosphere. Understanding why remains an important scientific question.

🔄

It Rotates

The Sun rotates at different rates depending on latitude because it is made of plasma rather than a rigid solid.

The Sun is approximately halfway through its main-sequence lifetime.
